Growing up in Libreville, Gabon, I had no critical opinion of the way information was displayed in the public space, simply because that was my native environment and at this point, I had nothing else to compare it to. With my interest for visual communication and graphic design increasing and a couple of years spent in Europe, I started putting in perspective the knowledge I was gathering with the situation in my home country. In Gabon, it is not infrequent to witness billboards, posters or store signs showing a lack of awareness of the proper way to showcase their message, and this is true even for government advertising campaigns.

You don’t need to be a graphic design master to notice the numerous flaws with all these billboards. Despite being issued by government agencies, none of them look like they took more than five minutes from start to finish : discutable font choices, white letters on white background, absence of hierarchy, unimaginative catchphrases, roughly integrated logos (when they are)…

Too many of these abominations are still visible alongside the country’s roads. Although graphic design software and resources became widely available and affordable in the last fifteen years, these ads have barely evolved since I was old enough to notice them. Two reasons can explain this:

- There is no professional degree or qualifying diploma that can be obtained in Gabon in the field of graphic design, no school, public or private, offers training in the field: those who work as graphic designers obtained their degrees abroad or learned by themselves, on the job.
- The people in charge of validating these billboards are, for the most part, too old-fashioned of disinterested to have an understanding of graphic design’s purpose, therefore they don’t have any problem with these billboards looking the way they look. Graphic design has little to no recognition as a profession, which is everything but surprising in a country that doesn’t train graphic designers.

### The exception to the rule

Strangely enough, there is one field of public advertising in Gabon that doesn’t suffer of the same shortcomings : political communication.

When election time comes, every party involved want to appear as clean, professional and distinguishable from the other as possible, thus spending considerable amounts of money in contracts with world renown advertising firms to deliver these rather bland but professional looking billboards (political advertising has its norms, but still). During election season you will be handed high quality flyers printed on thick and resistant paper, colorful t-shirts, fine Dutch wax fabric and you’ll see giant billboards covering most building facades. What if the burning enthusiasm that seizes the population at these moments had also something to do with our public space being inundated with tasteful and accurate advertising?

### Public advertising by the people, for the people

There is another type of public advertising that you will encounter very often while walking the streets of Libreville or many other African cities, a style that you might find very charming and picturesque because it’s rather unique to that part of the world. For lack of a better term, let’s call it “street advertising”.

These oil paintings and freehand drawn signs for barber shops, grocery stores, mechanics, tailors and bars constitute a major aspect of the visual landscape in the city. These signs are easy to recognize, they have their own codes, their own techniques and they are deeply rooted in the popular culture. Each one of these signs is associated with an activity, an environment, a scent, a personal experience, a memory… They are a core element of the visual identity of the country. If we had to define a graphic identity for Gabon, it would have to be inspired by these artworks above.

Gabon became an independent nation in 1960, which makes it a relatively young country where there’s still a lot of norms awaiting to be established; but Gabon is also a country where things only change when change is the last option before chaos. I strongly believe that if we’re able to build a visual culture in continuity of what already exists, a culture that would not be a copy-and-paste of the western norms of visual communication but would blend these norms with the local ones, ideas would be displayed in a familiar manner that wouldn’t make no concession to quality by ignorance, and graphic design would then be able to better serve its original mission.
