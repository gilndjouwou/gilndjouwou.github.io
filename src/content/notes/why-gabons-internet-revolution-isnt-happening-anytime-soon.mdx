In late 2008 I left my home country of Gabon to pursue my studies in Reims, France. I never had to live separately from my family and friends so at 19 years old, that was a life-changing move. I had to adapt to a whole new life, get used to the cold weather and grapple with intense homesickness every day. I was making phone calls to my family and friends multiple times every single day, and it took me a dozen of day and a couple of hundred euros spent on phone cards to realize that sooner or later, I would have to choose between eating and listening to the comforting voices of my loved ones.

Today, in 2016, I still live in Reims and I still have that need to talk with my relatives every single day, and it’s no longer a threat to my budget balance. Instead of standing in the freezing cold of winter in a phone booth, I just have to grab my smartphone, open one of the many apps allowing to make phone calls and boom, it’s done, fast and totally free of charge.

I don’t need to repeat what you can already hear everywhere: the internet is changing Africa, the internet is helping the continent’s development, the internet helps people do business better, the internet allows African to do anything the lack of infrastructures makes very hard or impossible to do. All of this is very true : as for the rest of the world, the internet is a life-changing revolution. But as enthusiast as these columnists are, they are often missing a key element: law-making. In Gabon for example, there is barely any legislation about internet security, no competent patents management authority, reliable traffic regulation, no clear laws on hacking or software licensing, even copyrighting doesn’t have a proper legal structure. And the reason for that? Politics. In Gabon as in many other African countries, everything is political, everything. Most of the times, a department like network and communication is ran by a pure career politician that isn’t technically proficient enough to know how things should work and make them work. A good illustration of that is the way Gabon handled the Mega case back in 2012 : the domain name of the new cloud storage service of Kim Dotcom was supposed to be hosted in Gabon (because of the .ga extension) and on no legal basis, the minister of communication made a press conference where he called cloud storage “cyber-crime” and said that a service that wasn’t even launched yet was already illegal. Now we all know that people would more than probably share copyrighted content on Mega, but come on.

It is more or less tight depending of the country, but in Gabon, there is still a choke-hold on law and policy making, freedom of entreprise and humans rights that makes it impossible for citizens to fully immerse themselves in what you would call the African internet revolution. It is an habit now to completely shut down the internet and all mobile communications during political elections and the eventual civil unrest, needless to say that this type of measure is a massive obstacle against any type of development.

> A related trend in recent years — which also demonstrates the power of the internet and mobile connectivity — >has been the shutting down of networks, or certain sites, during elections or moments of crisis. Well documented during the Arab spring, shutdowns have taken place already this year in Uganda, Chad, Republic of Congo, and Ghana, often seen as a democratic role model in Africa.

> […]“Now, when it comes to critical moments, you can’t arrest everyone in order to keep the story from getting out — so governments figure they will just shut the internet down. The telcos just shrug their shoulders. Many are powerful enough to do so, but I’ve not seen an attempt to put up a fight.”

> *["Can the internet reboot Africa?"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2016/jul/25/can-the-internet-reboot-africa)* The Guardian, Monday 25th July 2016 

Recently, Motherboard called Gabon’s internet curfew the ‘worst communications suppression since the Arab Spring’, and that wasn’t an exaggeration. Africa might be able to make it, despite its political leaders, but I don’t like to abandon a question of such great significance to uncertainty. The internet was completely shut down for five full days, then was available only during working hours for the next three weeks, causing millions of FCFA in losses and distress in the population, still traumatized by the post-electoral violences of late August/early September. A population under such stress don’t create, don’t consume, don’t participate in social life and can’t look forward to the future, no matter how bright it is promised to be.

In Gabon, 3G/4G coverage is known to be unreliable and laggy , when landline internet plans’s rates are unreachable for the average citizen. With a minimum wage of 80 000 FCFA (122€/134$) and a price of 20 000 FCFA for 2MB/s of theoretical download speed, Gabonese people will have to rely on internet cafés and sluggish mobile internet for a long time. This is why Facebook made lighter versions of its apps Facebook and Messenger; it’s an answer to a real consumers’ need (the Facebook Lite app on Android reached 100 millions of users in no time), but for how long will it stay a pressing need?

There will be no real change in countries like Gabon before our ruling class get its priorities straight and puts new policies in place. The gap that need to be closed between us and first world countries is not only infrastructural but also (and mostly) legal, ethical, and political, and if we don’t imprint that in our minds, we will hardly see any real change happening.
